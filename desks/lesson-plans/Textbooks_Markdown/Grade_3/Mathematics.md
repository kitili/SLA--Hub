# Chapter One
# Whole numbers

### Introduction

In this chapter, you will learn about whole numbers with more than one digit. You will recognise the place value of a digit in whole numbers, and also read and write whole numbers. You will also compare numbers and recognise sequences of whole numbers. The competencies developed will enable you to use numbers in different contexts.

### Place values in whole numbers

The place value of a digit depends on its position in a number. The place value increases from the right to the left of a number. The place value of a digit in a number can be read from right to left as follows:

(a) The first position from right represents **ones**.
(b) The second position from right represents **tens**.
(c) The third position from right represents **hundreds**.
(d) The fourth position from right represents **thousands**.
(e) The fifth position from right represents **ten thousands**.
(f) The sixth position from right represents **hundred thousands**.
(g) The seven position from right represents **millions**.

## Reading and writing whole numbers

Reading and writing whole numbers involves interpreting numerical symbols and expressing quantities in written form. In this section, you will learn the techniques for reading and writing whole numbers.

### Reading whole numbers

Reading whole numbers depends on the number of digits and the total value of the digits in the corresponding number. Start identifying the ones, tens, hundreds, thousands, and so on. Thereafter, read the number from left to right by considering the total value of the corresponding digit. That is, read a number beginning with a digit that has the highest place value. Numbers are read based on the value of the digits from left to right.

**Example 1**

Read the following numbers:
(a) 1001             (b) 1086             (c) 1100

**Answer**
(a) 1001 is read as one thousand and one.
(b) 1086 is read as one thousand and eighty-six.
(c) 1100 is read as one thousand and one hundred.

## Sequences of whole numbers

Numbers can be arranged in a defined pattern. When whole numbers are arranged in a defined pattern, they form a sequence. It is possible to determine the next numbers in a given sequence.

Carefully study the number of bottle tops in group A, B and C, then answer the questions that follow.

[diagram: Group A containing 2 bottle tops, Group B containing 5 bottle tops, Group C containing 8 bottle tops]

$$\text{Group A} \qquad\qquad\qquad \text{Group B} \qquad\qquad\qquad \text{Group C}$$

--- PAGE 40 ---

<!--folio:33-->

Group A has 2 bottle tops. Group B has 5 bottle tops, Group C has 8 bottle tops. The number of bottle tops in the groups can be listed as 2, 5, 8. There is an increase of 3 bottle tops from Group A to Group B. Similarly, there is an increase of 3 bottle tops from Group B to Group C. This means that 2, 5, 8, is a sequence of numbers. If you form another group of bottle tops the group will have 11 bottle tops. That is 8 + 3 = 11. In this case, the sequence will be 2, 5, 8, 11.

### Example 1

| | |

| 4 + 3 = 7 | 16 + 3 = 19 |
| 7 + 3 = 10 | 19 + 3 = 22 |
| 10 + 3 = 13 | 22 + 3 = 25 |

Therefore, the answer is 1, 4, 7, 10, 13, 16, 19, 22, 25.

### Measurements of length
Length is the distance between two points. It can be measured by using different tools. The measuring tools of length are of two types, namely non-standard and standard tools.

The use of non-standard measuring tools of length depends on the type of objects being measured. For example, the length of a chalkboard or table can be measured using non-standard tools such as handspan, stick, rope or footsteps.

## Addition of fractions with the same denominator

To add fractions with the same denominator simply add the numerators and keep the denominator the same.

### Example 1

2. Write in numerals the three-quarters of the circle.
3. Draw and shade a quarter of the circle.
4. Write the quarter of a circle in numerals.
5. Add the shaded parts of the circle.
6. Draw a shaded whole circle to represent the sum of three-quarters and one-quarter.

[diagram: Addition of circle sectors where three-quarters of a shaded circle plus one-quarter of a shaded circle equals a fully shaded circle divided into four quarters]

$$\frac{3}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{4}{4}$$

The answer is one shaded circle divided into four equal parts. To add fractions with the same denominator, add the numerators as you add ordinary numbers. The denominator does not change because the parts make up one thing. Thus, $\frac{3}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3+1}{4} = \frac{4}{4}$.

Therefore, $\frac{3}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{4}{4}$.

Thus, $\frac{13}{27} + \frac{7}{27} = \frac{13+7}{27} = \frac{20}{27}$.

Therefore, $\frac{13}{27} + \frac{7}{27} = \frac{20}{27}$.

### Word problems involving addition of fractions

#### Example 1
Yona filled $\frac{3}{9}$ of a tank with water, and Halima filled $\frac{2}{9}$ of the tank. What fraction of the tank did they fill?

**Solution**

Yona filled $\frac{3}{9}$ of the tank.

Halima filled $\frac{2}{9}$ of the tank.

Both of them filled: $\frac{3}{9} + \frac{2}{9} = \frac{3+2}{9}$
$= \frac{5}{9}$

Therefore, both Yona and Halima filled $\frac{5}{9}$ of the tank with water.

In total they used: $\frac{3}{8} + \frac{4}{8} = \frac{3+4}{8}$
$= \frac{7}{8}$

Therefore, Anna and John used $\frac{7}{8}$ of the sugar.

The previous steps can be illustrated as follows:

[diagram: A circle divided into three shaded parts, with an arrow indicating one part is being removed] - [diagram: One shaded part of the circle] = [diagram: Two shaded parts of the circle]

$$ \frac{3}{3} - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3} $$

To subtract fractions with the same denominator subtract the numerators as you subtract ordinary numbers. The denominator does not change because the parts make up one thing.

Therefore, $\frac{3}{3} - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$.

## Word problems involving subtraction of fractions

### Example 1

Five-eighths of a farm is planted eucalyptus trees. The rest of the farm is planted orange trees. What part of the farm is planted orange trees?

**Solution**

The whole farm is divided into 8 parts; same as $\frac{8}{8}$.

The area planted eucalyptus trees is $\frac{5}{8}$.

The area planted orange trees = whole farm – the area planted eucalyptus trees.

$$= \frac{8}{8} - \frac{5}{8}$$

$$= \frac{8 - 5}{8} = \frac{3}{8}$$

Therefore, the part of the farm planted oranges trees is $\frac{3}{8}$.
